# Go-to-Market

A go-to-market plan is what happens when the position from `01-positioning.md` has to survive contact with real channels, real budgets, and a real launch date. This file structures that translation.

## Messaging hierarchy

Everything a customer reads, in any channel, should trace back through the same chain. If a piece of copy can't be traced back to the positioning statement, it's either building a new position by accident, or it's noise.

```
Positioning statement (one sentence, from 01-positioning.md)
        |
Pillar messages (2-3 supporting ideas, each a facet of the position)
        |
Proof points (specific, provable facts backing each pillar)
        |
Channel adaptations (the same hierarchy, reshaped per channel's format and attention level)
```

**Working discipline:** before approving copy for any channel, ask which pillar it's expressing and which proof point backs it. Copy that can't answer both questions is drifting from the position, even if it's well-written.

## Launch phasing

Three phases, each with a different job. Collapsing them into one big push on launch day is the most common go-to-market mistake — it treats awareness, consideration, and habit-formation as the same problem, when they require different messages entirely.

**Pre-launch (seed/teaser).** The job here is not to explain the product — it's to make the eventual announcement land on prepared ground. Steve Hayden's Apple "1984" launch (see `05-copywriting-canon.md`) worked partly because Chiat/Day built anticipation for a single unmissable moment rather than drip-feeding features beforehand. For most brand-manager launches without that kind of budget, pre-launch is smaller: seeding the problem the product solves (not the product itself) with the audience segment most likely to become early advocates.

**Launch (the big swing moment).** This is where the single-minded proposition gets its clearest, loudest expression, ideally concentrated rather than spread thin across a long slow ramp. A launch budget spread evenly across eight weeks usually underperforms the same budget concentrated into a shorter, unmissable window — attention is won by intensity relative to the moment, not by total exposure over time.

**Post-launch (sustain/always-on).** This is where `02-brand-voice-system.md`'s always-on and pillar content categories take over from campaign spikes. The launch moment builds initial awareness; sustain content is what converts that awareness into the ladder position actually sticking in the customer's head over the following months. A brand that launches loud and then goes quiet loses the position gains almost as fast as it made them.

## Channel mix logic tied to ladder position

Channel strategy shouldn't be decided by channel performance benchmarks alone — it should follow from the ladder position established in `01-positioning.md`:

- **If you're defending a leader position:** broad reach, category-defining channels, media weight that reinforces "this is simply what the category looks like." Leaders can afford (and usually need) breadth over precision.
- **If you're a follower hunting a specific creneau (gap):** concentrated, precision-targeted channels that reach the specific segment who cares about the gap you're claiming — broad reach wastes budget on people who were never going to be won by a narrow, differentiated position anyway.
- **If you're repositioning the competition or making a provocative claim (see the Sinclair technique in `05-copywriting-canon.md`):** earned and owned channels that can carry a longer, more explained version of the claim tend to outperform paid channels that only have room for the provocation without the substantiation — a provocative claim without room to back it up in the same channel invites backlash rather than persuasion.

**Cross-functional checklist:**
- [ ] Sales enablement briefed with the same positioning language, not a separately-invented pitch
- [ ] CRM/lifecycle messaging updated to reflect the new position, not left running old messaging in parallel
- [ ] Customer service given the same claims and the same tone-of-voice exceptions (see `02-brand-voice-system.md`) so post-purchase experience doesn't contradict the launch promise
- [ ] Retail/trade or partner-facing materials aligned, if applicable — a launch that looks different on-shelf or in a partner's hands than it does in paid media undermines the position it just spent budget building
- [ ] Legal/medical/compliance review complete for any comparative, health, or financial claim before the launch date, not scheduled around it

**Success metrics and check-in cadence:** *(tie back to the launch objective; set an explicit date to run the `06-offer-and-campaign-audit.md` post-mortem, not an open-ended "we'll look at it eventually")*
