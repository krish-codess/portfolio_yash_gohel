# The Copywriting Canon

Every technique below is tied to a real, documented campaign or working method, described in this skill's own words. Nothing here is a verbatim quote from *The Copy Book* (D&AD/TASCHEN) or any other source — it's a practical translation of real, historically documented approaches, meant to be used, not just admired. Where a detail couldn't be independently confirmed with confidence, the person was left out rather than guessed at.

Use this file two ways: as a reference when you need a specific kind of copy (find the technique that matches your problem), and as a diagnostic when copy isn't working (find the technique it's failing to use).

## 1. The long-copy discipline — David Abbott, Neil French

David Abbott, co-founder of what became Abbott Mead Vickers BBDO, built a reputation on long, careful copy for The Economist, Volvo, Sainsbury's, and the RSPCA, working from a discipline rather than a style: research before writing, and don't start writing until you have more to say than will fit — the cutting comes after, not instead of, having something real to cut down. He read every draft aloud, on the belief that copy which doesn't sound right spoken aloud won't read right silently either. Underlying all of it was a simple respect for the reader's intelligence — his Sainsbury's work in particular treated shoppers as people making informed choices, not targets to be tricked, which was a genuine departure from most retail advertising of its era.

Neil French, who built his reputation across Ogilvy, Batey, and The Ball Partnership in Asia, took long copy somewhere more theatrical — his Chivas Regal and Kaminomoto Hair Tonic work, and a fictional beer called XO he wrote about so persuasively that readers began asking bartenders for a beer that didn't exist. French's specific discipline: never write in an invented "brand voice." Every piece sounds like one specific, opinionated person talking directly to the reader — his own voice, deployed on the client's behalf, not a committee-safe abstraction of what a brand "should" sound like.

**Use this when:** the product has real, specific, defensible substance and the audience is genuinely willing to spend thirty seconds with the category (finance, health, spirits, anything considered). **Diagnostic question:** does this copy sound like it was written by a specific person with a point of view, or by a brand-voice template?

## 2. The creative leap — Dave Trott

Dave Trott's career-long argument, developed across decades at agencies including BMP and GGT and taught since in his own writing, is that most advertising fails not from bad execution but from answering the brief too literally. The brief asks a rational question; the average team gives a rational answer; the answer is forgettable because every competitor's rational answer to the same rational question looks roughly the same. The creative leap is the deliberate move away from the obvious, literal answer toward the answer that comes from questioning the brief's own assumptions rather than accepting them.

**Practical version:** before approving a first-round concept, ask whether it answers the brief in the most obvious way a competent team would answer it. If yes, it's not wrong, but it's not yet a leap — push once more before locking it in.

## 3. The single big swing — Steve Hayden

Steve Hayden, copywriter on Chiat/Day's 1984 launch spot for the Apple Macintosh (working alongside creative director Lee Clow and art director Brent Thomas), didn't describe the product's features. The commercial staged one unmissable moment — an Orwellian "Big Blue" conformity shattered by a single act of rebellion — that recast the entire category's meaning in sixty seconds, aired nationally exactly once, during the Super Bowl. The product itself was never shown.

**Use this when:** you're launching into a category with an entrenched leader and a real, once-only moment (a launch window, a cultural moment) to reframe what the category means rather than compete feature-by-feature. **Diagnostic question:** if you removed every feature callout from this launch work, is there still a moment left, or was the "big idea" actually just a list?

## 4. Zag against category convention — John Hegarty, BBH

Hegarty's "When the world zigs, zag" line for Levi's became successful enough that the client's black sheep imagery was adopted as BBH's own agency logo — proof that a deliberate, ownable opposite to category convention can become identity, not just a single execution. See `04-creative-development.md` for the full method of mapping category defaults and finding the ownable opposite.

## 5. Cause and behavior-change copy — Indra Sinha, John Bevins

Indra Sinha, working across Ogilvy & Mather and Collett Dickenson Pearce, built his reputation on campaigning work for Amnesty International and later the Bhopal Medical Appeal — copy that earned him recognition as one of Britain's most respected copywriters by trading persuasion tricks for unflinching, specific human truth, trusting that the fact itself was more powerful than any device layered on top of it.

John Bevins, founder of his own Sydney agency, built an entire practice around public behavior-change work — Australia's Random Breath Testing campaign, credited with a 22% drop in road deaths against the prior six-year average, and the Quit For Life anti-smoking campaigns, both stark, direct, unglamorous, and provably effective at changing behavior rather than just building awareness.

**Use this when:** the objective is genuine behavior change (health, safety, public good) rather than preference or brand affinity — directly relevant ground for healthtech and wellness brand work. **Diagnostic question:** would this copy still be true and still work if you removed every persuasive flourish and left only the plain fact? If the plain fact alone is more powerful than the dressed-up version, you're over-writing it.

## 6. Provocation as reframe — Jeremy Sinclair, Saatchi & Saatchi

Jeremy Sinclair's 1969 "pregnant man" ad for the Health Education Council (via Cramer Saatchi, the agency that preceded Saatchi & Saatchi) reframed contraceptive responsibility with a single startling image and the line "Would you be more careful if it was you that got pregnant?" — no lecture, no statistics, one reframe. Nearly three decades later, his "New Labour, New Danger" poster for the Conservative Party used demonic eyes glowing behind a red curtain to make an abstract political warning viscerally immediate. Both work the same way: take a familiar issue, find the single visual or verbal reframe that makes an audience see it freshly, and say nothing else.

**Use this when:** the audience has heard the category's message so many times it's become wallpaper, and the goal is to make them actually see the issue again, not just hear it repeated louder.

## 7. Concept before craft — Tim Delaney, Steve Dunn, Mike Lescarbeau

Timberland's 1991 "evolution" print campaign — built around the line that Timberland gives outerwear back the years of evolution it took Man to lose his weatherproofing — was conceived by Leagas Delaney founder Tim Delaney with art director Steve Dunn, and the specific execution was written by Mike Lescarbeau. The lesson in that division of labor is the point: the campaign idea (the strategic concept) came first and came from the founders setting direction, and the copywriting's job was to serve that idea with precision, not to invent a new one underneath it. Lescarbeau's own broader career — BMW, Lee Jeans, and Jim Beam work built at Fallon in Minneapolis — reinforced the same discipline: distinctive copy in service of a clear idea, not decoration layered onto a vague one.

**Diagnostic question:** if you can't state the campaign's central idea in one sentence before you start writing headlines, you're not ready to write headlines yet — you're going to write five clever ones for five different ideas, and none of them will compound.

## 8. Brand voice at scale, every day — Dan Germain, Nick Asbury

Covered in full in `02-brand-voice-system.md`: Dan Germain's work for Innocent Drinks proved that relentless, small-scale voice consistency (bottle copy, packaging, everyday social replies) builds more brand equity over time than occasional big campaigns alone. Nick Asbury's more recent practice treats "verbal identity" — a brand's language system — with the same rigor traditionally reserved for visual identity: documented, tested, and applied consistently rather than left to whoever's writing that day.

## 9. The tagline discipline — Dan Wieden

Dan Wieden, co-founder of Wieden+Kennedy, is best known for "Just Do It" — a line that works because it isn't a product description, it's a behavior prompt, applicable to a runner, an athlete, or anyone facing resistance, entirely independent of any specific shoe's features. The discipline behind it generalizes into a hard test for any tagline: could a direct competitor's name be substituted in and have the line still make sense? If yes, the line is describing the category, not the brand, and it isn't doing its job yet.

## 10. The craft codifiers — Luke Sullivan, Bob Levenson

Some of the most useful copywriting knowledge doesn't come from a single campaign — it comes from practitioners who stopped to write the discipline down. Luke Sullivan's *Hey Whipple, Squeeze This* became one of the most widely used craft references in the industry precisely because it codifies what separates a genuinely strong idea from decorated mediocrity, one blunt chapter at a time. Bob Levenson, part of the generation of DDB writers who worked in the Volkswagen advertising Bill Bernbach's agency became famous for (Levenson's own credited lines include VW's "It makes your house look bigger" and Mobil's "We want you to live"), later wrote and spoke extensively about that era's founding discipline: one idea per ad, cut everything that doesn't serve it, and treat plain honesty as a persuasion strategy, not just an ethical position.

## 11. The proof-point-as-person — Ed McCabe

Ed McCabe, co-founder of Scali, McCabe, Sloves, turned Frank Perdue himself into Perdue Chicken's proof point with the line "It takes a tough man to make a tender chicken" — an unglamorous commodity category made personal, ownable, and instantly repeatable by tying the brand's promise to a specific, recognizable human being rather than an abstract quality claim.

**Use this when:** the category is a commodity and every competitor is making a similar rational claim — a real, specific person (founder, expert, customer) willing to stand behind the claim publicly is often more differentiating than a better-written version of the same generic promise.

## 12. The emotional case for rational categories — Janet Kestin

Janet Kestin, Co-Chief Creative Officer of Ogilvy Toronto for thirteen years, was part of the leadership behind Dove's Campaign for Real Beauty — proof that even a category as functionally commoditized as soap and skincare can be repositioned through pure emotional truth-telling rather than another round of product-attribute claims. The campaign's films ("Evolution" among them) didn't describe the product at all; they told the audience an uncomfortable truth about the beauty industry's own norms, and let the brand's implied position follow from that.

**Use this when:** every competitor in a rational, feature-driven category is making similar functional claims, and the opening isn't a better feature, it's an emotional truth nobody else in the category is willing to say out loud.

## Headline diagnostic

Run any headline or campaign line through these lenses before you call it finished:

- **The Abbott test:** could this survive being read aloud to someone whose intelligence you respect, without sounding like it's talking down to them?
- **The Trott test:** is this the obvious, literal answer to the brief, or did it take the leap past the obvious answer?
- **The zag test:** cover the logo — does this still look and sound different from what every competitor in the category would produce from the same brief?
- **The Wieden test:** swap in a competitor's name. Does the line still make sense? If yes, rewrite it.
- **The McCabe test:** is there a real, specific, ownable proof point in here, or is this a claim any brand in the category could also make?
- **The plain-fact test (Sinha/Bevins):** if you stripped out every persuasive device and stated the underlying fact plainly, would it be more powerful than the version in front of you? If yes, you're over-writing it.
