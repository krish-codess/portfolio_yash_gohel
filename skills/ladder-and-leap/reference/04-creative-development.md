# Creative Development & Review

Ideas in this file are an original synthesis inspired by John Hegarty's *Hegarty on Creativity: There Are No Rules* (2021), reframed specifically for the brand manager's side of the table — the person briefing, reviewing, and signing off on creative work, not the person making it. Read the book for the full argument, told in Hegarty's own voice through fifty short provocations. This file is a working framework, not a summary of it.

## The brand manager's actual job in the creative process

Hegarty's central claim is that creativity has no fixed rules, only guidelines worth questioning — which is uncomfortable for brand managers, whose entire job usually runs on guidelines. The resolution isn't to abandon brand guidelines. It's to understand what they're for: guidelines exist to protect the strategy, not to control the execution. A brand manager who edits tone-of-voice guidelines into a straitjacket that dictates sentence structure has confused the map for the territory. Guidelines should make the position impossible to miss and leave the *expression* of it as open as the work needs to be.

## Never hand over a blank page

Hegarty describes the blank page as one of the most paralyzing moments in any creative process — and for a brand manager, the practical translation is blunt: if your creative team is staring at a blank page, that's usually not a talent problem, it's a brief problem. `03-creative-brief.md` exists precisely to prevent this — a tight single-minded proposition gives a creative team a wall to push off, not a void to fill. A brand manager who hands over a vague brief and then blames the resulting vague work is skipping the step where most of the actual thinking needed to happen.

## Chaos vs. process

Every creative organization leans toward one of two failure modes: total chaos (no structure, ideas thrown around, greatness hoped for rather than built) or over-process (so much system that nothing surprising can survive it). Hegarty's resolution is to apply process to the edges of creative work — deadlines, budgets, mandatories, the brief itself — and leave the middle of the process open. As a brand manager, that means:

- Lock the brief, the deadline, and the mandatories early, and don't relitigate them mid-process (that's process working correctly).
- Do not attempt to control *how* the idea gets found in between — resist the instinct to prescribe format, structure, or "just make it look like the last campaign that worked."
- If you find yourself scheduling a "brainstorm meeting" at a fixed time expecting an idea to appear on cue, recognize that as a process failure, not a chaos failure — real ideas rarely arrive to a 10am slot.

## Zag: mapping the category's default settings

One of the most immediately usable ideas in the book is captured in a real, verifiable campaign: Hegarty and BBH's "When the world zigs, zag" line for Levi's, which became successful enough that the client adopted a black sheep as BBH's own logo. The underlying method, generalized for any brand manager:

1. Literally list what every competitor in your category currently does — their dominant color, their tone, their claim structure, their imagery, their format.
2. For each item on that list, ask whether doing the deliberate opposite is *ownable* — not just different for its own sake, but different in a way that still serves your actual position.
3. Where the answer is yes, that's your zag. Where it's just contrarianism with no connection to the position, it's noise, not a zag — Hegarty's own caveat throughout the book is that freshness must still serve a truth, not replace one.

## Fresh, not original — and the honest test for it

Hegarty is direct that nothing is fully original — every idea borrows, blends, and reinterprets something that came before it, and chasing "originality" as a goal is a trap. The more useful bar is freshness: does this stop someone, does it make them see the category differently, does it move them to act. Translated into a brand manager's creative-review vocabulary, ask the work three questions before anything else:

1. Would this stop someone scrolling or flipping past, on its own, with no context?
2. Does it make the audience look at the category (or the problem) even slightly differently than they did thirty seconds ago?
3. Does it point toward an action, or does it just sit there being liked?

## Point of view over consensus

Work with a real point of view behind it reads differently than work assembled to avoid offending anyone in a review chain — and a review process with too many voices reliably produces the second kind. Hegarty's collaboration chapters make an unfashionable but well-earned point: brainstorming with a large group tends toward consensus, and consensus tends toward average, because the safest idea in the room is the one nobody objects to, not the one that's actually strongest. The practical brand-manager application:

- Keep the creative review small. Every additional voice in the room with a vote (not just an opinion) pulls the work toward the average of everyone's taste, not the strength of the strategy.
- Route feedback through a single creative lead rather than a committee giving notes directly to a junior writer — mixed, contradictory notes from five stakeholders is the single most common way strong work gets sanded down to nothing.
- When two strong, differently-skilled people build something together (Hegarty's own example: art director and copywriter, or any complementary pairing), the friction between different starting points is often *where* the idea comes from — this is different from a large group converging toward safety, and shouldn't be pruned the same way.

## The creative review filter

Run finished or near-finished work through these seven questions before sign-off. They're designed to catch the two most common brand-manager failure modes: killing genuinely strong work out of nervousness, and approving safe, forgettable work because it didn't upset anyone in the review.

1. **Truth** — Is there one real, specific truth this work is built on, statable in a sentence? (Not a category platitude — an actual, specific truth about this product or this audience.)
2. **Strategy fit** — Does it say the single-minded proposition from the brief, or does it say something more generic that any brand in the category could also claim?
3. **Freshness** — Would this stop someone, on its own, with no context? (See above.)
4. **Zag check** — Does it look, sound, or read differently than the category default, or does it blend into competitor work if you cover the logo?
5. **Voice fit** — Does it sound like your brand specifically, per `02-brand-voice-system.md`, or could it belong to any brand with a similar tone description?
6. **Fearlessness** — Is this the safe, consensus-shaped version of the idea, or the version with an actual point of view? If everyone in the review liked it immediately with no discomfort, that's worth naming out loud, not treating as a good sign by default.
7. **Memorability without the logo** — Cover the logo. Is there still something distinctive enough to remember, or does the work rely entirely on the brand name to mean anything?

A piece of work that fails "strategy fit" needs a rewrite. A piece of work that fails "fearlessness" because the room got nervous, not because the strategy was wrong, needs a brand manager willing to defend it one more round before it gets sanded down to safe.

## Good is the enemy of great — and knowing when to stop

The same discipline that prevents work from being average also has a failure mode in the other direction: endless rounds of notes chasing a theoretical "great" can wear a genuinely good idea down to nothing, or blow a deadline chasing a version of perfect that doesn't exist. Two practical guardrails:

- Cap review rounds before the brief is even sent — three rounds is a reasonable default for most work, with a named decision-maker who breaks ties after that.
- Distinguish notes that fix a strategy problem (always worth another round) from notes that are really just personal taste (a maximum of one round, and ideally resolved by the creative lead, not re-opened by the brand manager every time).
