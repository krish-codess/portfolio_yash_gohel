# Offer & Campaign Audit: The Five Fit Checks

An original scoring framework for stress-testing an offer, campaign, or piece of creative before it launches — or for diagnosing one that already ran and underperformed. It's built specifically for brand-side marketing (positioning, campaigns, brand offers), not for direct-response funnel construction — if you need a direct-response offer-construction framework (pricing, guarantees, bonus stacking), that's a different job than this file does.

## The five checks

Score each dimension 1–5. A campaign or offer scoring below 3 on any single dimension has a specific, nameable weakness — don't average the scores together into one number and call it "fine." A 4.6 average hiding one dimension scored at 1 is not a healthy campaign, it's a campaign with one landmine in it.

### 1. Category Fit — does it make sense against the ladder?

Does this offer or campaign make sense given the rung your brand actually holds (per `01-positioning.md`)? A challenger brand running leader-style broad-reassurance messaging, or a leader running challenger-style attack messaging, is fighting the wrong fight for its position.

- **1** — The campaign's tone and claims assume a market position the brand doesn't actually hold.
- **3** — The campaign is plausible for the brand's rung but doesn't actively reinforce it.
- **5** — The campaign could only have been made by a brand at exactly this rung, defending or claiming it deliberately.

### 2. Truth Fit — is there a real reason to believe?

Every claim in the offer or campaign should trace back to something provably true about the product, the pricing, or the guarantee — not an aspirational adjective. This is the check most likely to create legal or regulatory exposure if skipped, particularly for health and financial claims.

- **1** — Claims are aspirational language with no specific, provable backing.
- **3** — Claims are true but stated generically, with no distinctive proof point.
- **5** — Every major claim has a specific, provable, ideally exclusive reason to believe behind it.

### 3. Voice Fit — does it sound like the brand, or like generic category-speak?

Run the copy against `02-brand-voice-system.md`'s voice matrix and "say it, don't describe it" examples. Copy that could be swapped into a competitor's campaign with a find-and-replace on the brand name has failed this check regardless of how well-written it is line by line.

- **1** — Reads as generic category boilerplate; could belong to any competitor.
- **3** — Recognizably on-brand in isolated lines, inconsistent across the full piece.
- **5** — Unmistakably this brand's voice throughout, including in the fine print and mandatories.

### 4. Channel Fit — does the format match how the audience actually encounters it?

An idea can be strategically and creatively correct and still fail because it was built for the wrong format — a message that needs thirty seconds of attention built for a five-second skippable pre-roll slot, or a visual-first idea forced into a channel that's mostly consumed with sound off.

- **1** — The core idea depends on conditions (sound on, full attention, long dwell time) the primary channel doesn't reliably provide.
- **3** — The idea works in its primary channel but wasn't meaningfully adapted for secondary channels it also runs in.
- **5** — The idea was built with its actual consumption context in mind from the brief stage, and adapts without losing its core meaning across every channel it runs in.

### 5. Distinctiveness Fit — the blind swap test

Cover the logo and any brand-specific color or asset. Would a stranger, seeing only the message, guess correctly which brand made this? If a direct competitor's logo would fit just as naturally, the campaign is building the category, not the brand — which may still be a legitimate short-term choice, but should be a deliberate one, not an accident.

- **1** — A competitor's logo would fit the work at least as well as yours does.
- **3** — Brand-specific enough to feel "off" with a competitor's logo, but not memorable enough to be recalled without seeing the logo again.
- **5** — Distinctive enough (asset, structure, voice, or idea) that the audience could identify the brand even with the logo removed.

## Red flags to check for regardless of score

These are common enough, and damaging enough, to check explicitly rather than trusting the five-check score to catch them:

- **Line-extension creep** — an offer or campaign stretching the brand name onto a claim it hasn't earned yet (see the line-extension trap in `01-positioning.md`).
- **Brief-by-committee** — a campaign that tries to satisfy every internal stakeholder's pet request instead of the single-minded proposition it was briefed against (see `03-creative-brief.md`).
- **Chasing fashion instead of building distinctive assets** — a format, aesthetic, or platform trend adopted because it's currently popular rather than because it serves the position; fashion-chasing campaigns date fast and rarely compound.
- **Mistaking awareness for positioning** — a campaign that successfully made more people aware of the brand without changing what they believe the brand stands for. Awareness lift and position lift are different metrics; a campaign can win the first and completely miss the second.
- **Unsubstantiated comparative or health/financial claims** — anything that implies a comparison to a named or implied competitor, or a health/financial outcome, needs a specific, documented reason to believe before it ships, not after a challenge.

## Post-mortem template (after a campaign has run)

Use this once real performance data exists, whether the campaign beat or missed its goal — a post-mortem run only on failures teaches you nothing about what actually worked and why.

**What moved, and by how much?** Tie back to the original objective's behavior-change metric from the brief, not to vanity metrics that happened to move.

**Which of the Five Fit Checks scored lowest going in, and did that turn out to predict the outcome?** If Voice Fit was scored a 2 pre-launch and the campaign underperformed on brand recall, that's a validated signal for the framework, and a specific lesson for the next brief — not just a general "do better next time."

**What would a rerun change, specifically?** Not "make it better" — one specific, nameable change (a different reason to believe, a different channel allocation, a tighter proposition).

**What should become a standing rule, not just a one-off fix?** If the same failure mode (chasing a fashionable format, a brief with three objectives, a claim that needed legal review it didn't get) shows up in more than one post-mortem, it belongs in `03-creative-brief.md`'s failure-mode list or this file's red-flag list as a permanent check, not a lesson re-learned every cycle.
