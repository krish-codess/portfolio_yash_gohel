# Positioning

Frameworks in this file are original syntheses inspired by Al Ries and Jack Trout's *Positioning: The Battle for Your Mind* (1981/2001), adapted for practical brand-manager use. Read the book itself for the full argument — this is a working toolkit, not a substitute.

## The core idea, restated

Positioning is not something you do to a product. It's something you do to a gap in a customer's head.

Every category is already crowded with more brands than any customer can hold in mind. Ries and Trout called this the overcommunicated environment: more messages competing for less attention every year, which means the average customer's mind defends itself by simplifying — ranking a small number of options per category and ignoring the rest. Your job isn't to "communicate more" into that noise. It's to find the one slot your brand can actually occupy in a customer's already-oversimplified mental map, and then say the one thing that claims it.

This has a blunt implication most brand teams resist: a position is decided far more by what's already in the customer's head than by what's in your brand deck. You can't out-argue an existing belief. You can only find the belief you can credibly own, or the belief you can credibly attack.

## The ladder

Inside a customer's head, every category is organized as a ladder — a small number of ranked rungs, usually three or fewer that a customer can name without effort. Ask anyone to name a rental car company and most will produce two names, maybe three, in a rough order. That order is the ladder, and it exists whether or not your brand appears on it.

Two brand-manager exercises follow directly from this:

**1. Map the real ladder, not the ladder you wish existed.** For your category, ask five to ten actual customers (not colleagues) to name the brands they think of, in order, with no prompting. Do this before you write a single word of strategy. Most brand teams are positioning against a ladder that exists only in their own org chart.

**2. Locate your actual rung, honestly.** Brands routinely overestimate their own rung. If you're not top-of-mind in category research, you are not the leader in the customer's head, regardless of your market share on a spreadsheet. Startups running with fintech- or healthtech-adjacent budgets especially tend to conflate internal conviction with external position — the ladder only cares about what's outside the building.

Once you know your real rung, you have three strategic choices, not one:

- **Fight for a higher rung on the existing ladder.** Expensive, slow, and only works if the current leader is vulnerable (declining trust, a real product gap, a generational shift in the buyer). This is the hardest and most overchosen strategy in brand marketing — most "challenger brand" plans are secretly this, badly disguised.
- **Own an empty rung.** Find the attribute or use-case the ladder hasn't organized around yet, and become the answer to a question customers weren't yet asking in that shape. This is cheaper and faster, but only works if the empty rung is one customers will actually care about — an empty rung nobody wants is not a position, it's a hiding place.
- **Build a new ladder entirely.** Reframe the category so a different comparison set applies (a supplement brand that competes with "your evening wind-down routine" instead of with other supplements). High-reward, high-risk, and usually only available to genuinely category-defining products.

## Leader vs. follower

The single biggest strategic error brand managers make is running follower plays with leader budgets, or leader plays with follower budgets. The two require opposite behavior:

**If you're the leader (top rung, and the customer research confirms it):** your job is defense, not offense. Reinforce the original category belief that put you on top rather than chasing every attacker's new angle. Ries and Trout's sharpest leader-strategy point: a leader who panics and copies a challenger's new idea is doing the challenger's positioning work for them, for free, by legitimizing the very language the challenger invented. If a smaller rival launches on a new attribute, the leader's strongest move is often silence, or a broad umbrella response that covers every future attacker rather than a specific rebuttal to one.

**If you're a follower (any rung below the top, honestly assessed):** the leader's rung is not available to you no matter how good your product is — you cannot out-shout an incumbent on the exact ground they already own. What you're actually hunting for is a *creneau* (Ries and Trout's term, borrowed from French: literally a niche, a gap not yet claimed) — a real, uncontested rung a real customer segment cares about. Finding one usually means being willing to say what you are *not*, loudly, before you say what you are — the follower's version of positioning almost always requires narrowing before it can widen.

## Repositioning the competition

Sometimes the fastest way to open a gap isn't repositioning yourself — it's repositioning what's already sitting at the top of the ladder. Ries and Trout's own textbook example: rather than trying to out-market aspirin on aspirin's terms, Tylenol's positioning worked by surfacing a true but previously unsaid fact about aspirin (stomach irritation, bleeding risk for some users) that recast the entire category's leader as having a hidden cost. The product didn't change. The customer's belief about the *incumbent* changed, and a gap opened underneath it.

**Brand-manager prompt to run this play honestly:** what does the market currently and genuinely believe about the category leader that, if surfaced accurately and fairly, would open room for a real alternative? This only works if the claim is true and provable — repositioning the competition on a false or exaggerated claim is not a growth hack, it's a legal and trust liability, especially in regulated categories like health and finance.

## The line-extension trap

Ries and Trout's most-quoted caution, and the one brand managers ignore most often under internal pressure: stretching one brand name across too many products dilutes the very specificity that made the name mean something. A name that stands for one clear thing in a customer's head is worth more than a name spread thin across five things, each communicated at a fifth of the strength.

**A practical test before a line extension ships:** can a customer state, in one sentence and without hesitation, what the parent brand name now means, after the extension? If the honest answer requires a longer sentence than it did before the extension, the extension is costing you position equity even if it's growing short-term revenue. This doesn't mean never extend — it means extending is a withdrawal from an equity account, not a free line of credit, and someone should be tracking the balance.

## The power of naming

In an overcommunicated environment, the name is doing more strategic work than most brand teams budget time for. A name that already describes the benefit gets a running start into the customer's head; a name that requires explanation is fighting uphill from word one.

**A naming checklist for new products or sub-brands:**
- Said out loud, cold, to someone unfamiliar with the category, does it communicate anything, or does it require the tagline to do all the work?
- Is it ownable — could you trademark the specific *use* of this word in this category, or is it so generic five competitors could use a version of it tomorrow?
- Does it survive translation/negotiation across your actual markets, or does it carry an unintended meaning in a language or market you operate in?
- If it's an initialism or acronym, can a new customer reconstruct what it stands for without being told? (Ries and Trout's "no-name trap": brands that shorten a long name to initials to save space usually save nothing, because the initials only mean something to people who already knew the long name.)

## Positioning worksheet

Use this as a working document, not a one-time exercise — revisit it whenever the competitive set shifts.

**1. Category ladder map**

| Rung | Brand | Why they hold this rung (in the customer's words, not yours) |

| 2 | | |
| 3 | | |
| — | *(your brand, honestly placed)* | |

**2. Position audit — six questions, answered in one sentence each**
1. What rung do we actually hold today, per real customer input (not internal belief)?
2. What rung do we want, and is it a fight-for-it, own-it, or build-it situation?
3. Whose current position do we have to weaken, avoid, or reframe to get there?
4. Do we have the proof (product truth, budget, time) to make this stick, or are we buying a rung we can't afford to hold?
5. Will this organization still be saying the same thing in three years, or does this position have a shelf life shorter than the campaign that will announce it?
6. Does the creative work currently in market actually say this position, or does it say something more generic that any competitor could also claim?

**3. One-line positioning statement**

> For **[target customer]**, who **[has this need/frustration]**, **[brand]** is the **[category]** that **[unique, provable benefit]** — because **[reason to believe]** — unlike **[primary alternative]**, which **[their real limitation, stated fairly]**.

Write it, then delete every adjective that isn't load-bearing. A positioning statement with more than one claim in it is not a position, it's a wish list.
