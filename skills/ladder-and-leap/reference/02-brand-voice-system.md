# Brand Voice System

Positioning decides what you say. Voice decides how it sounds every single time you say it — in the ad, but also in the app notification, the shipping email, the customer-service macro, and the caption nobody thought needed a brand review. Most brands invest voice effort into the 5% of copy that runs as paid campaigns and leave the other 95% to whoever's typing. That other 95% is where a customer actually forms their sense of who you are.

## Why small copy compounds

Dan Germain built Innocent Drinks' reputation for warmth almost entirely through unglamorous copy — bottle labels, packaging asides, social replies — written with the same chatty, slightly silly, unmistakably human personality applied relentlessly, every single day, at a scale far larger than the brand's occasional big campaigns. The lesson for brand managers: voice consistency on low-stakes, high-frequency copy builds more brand equity over a year than one perfectly voiced hero campaign, because customers encounter the small copy far more often than they encounter the campaign.

If your brand guidelines only govern hero campaign copy, you don't have a voice system — you have a campaign style guide. A real system covers the email footer and the error message too.

## The voice matrix

Most tone-of-voice documents fail because they describe voice with adjectives ("friendly, bold, trustworthy") that every competitor's document also uses, and that give a writer no actual decision to make. A working voice system is a set of sliders, each anchored with real examples of what "too far" looks like in both directions — closer to what Nick Asbury's school of verbal-identity work treats as a discipline as rigorous as visual identity, not a vibe.

Set 3–4 sliders. More than that and nobody remembers them; fewer and you haven't actually made a decision.

| Dial | Too far left | Your setting | Too far right |
|---|---|---|---|
| Formal ←→ Casual | Sounds like a legal disclaimer | | Sounds like a text from a friend, loses authority |
| Serious ←→ Playful | No personality, forgettable | | Undermines trust in a health/finance/serious-stakes moment |
| Expert ←→ Peer | Talks down, jargon-heavy | | Sounds like it doesn't actually know the category |
| Reserved ←→ Bold | Says nothing memorable | | Claims things the product can't back up |

For a regulated or trust-sensitive category (health, finance — common ground for this skill's intended users), the *Serious ←→ Playful* dial in particular needs a hard-coded exception list: which moments (diagnosis, a failed payment, an error state) are never allowed to use the playful register regardless of general brand tone. Write that exception list down. It's the single most common voice failure in healthtech and fintech brands — a chatty brand voice showing up in a moment that needed to sound like it was taking the customer seriously.

## "Say it, don't describe it"

The most useful thing a voice document can contain is not a list of adjectives — it's five to ten real example sentences, written in-voice, covering the situations your copy actually has to handle: a product announcement, an apology, an error message, a celebratory moment, a moment of bad news. A writer who has never met your brand can replicate a sentence far more reliably than they can replicate an adjective.

## Content system: cadence, not just calendar

A content system built only as a calendar (what posts when) tends to collapse into filling slots. A content system built around cadence *types* holds up better under pressure and gives a brand manager a faster answer to "what do we make this week":

- **Pillar content** — the handful of themes the brand owns and returns to regardless of news cycle or trend. Should map directly to the positioning statement's proof points (see `01-positioning.md`) — if a pillar doesn't support the position, question why it's a pillar.
- **Always-on** — the low-effort, high-frequency content that keeps the account alive between campaigns: replies, small observations, UGC reshares. This is where the Germain principle above does its compounding work.
- **Campaign spikes** — the planned bursts tied to launches, seasonal moments, or paid pushes. Should be the minority of total output, not the majority — a content plan that's mostly spikes has no baseline personality between them.

**Quick ratio check:** if pillar + always-on content is less than roughly 60% of what you actually published last month, the brand is probably only showing up as a personality during campaigns and going quiet the rest of the time — which is the opposite of the small-copy-compounds principle above.

## Consistency audit

Run this across channels at least twice a year, or any time ownership of a channel changes hands (new agency, new hire, new platform).

1. Pull the last 10 pieces of copy from each touchpoint: paid ad, organic social, email/CRM, packaging or in-product copy, customer service macros, website.
2. Read them back to back, with brand names redacted if possible. Could a stranger tell they all came from the same brand?
3. Flag any touchpoint where the voice reads like a different, more generic brand wrote it — this is almost always CRM/lifecycle and customer service, the channels furthest from the marketing team's direct oversight and the ones customers read most often.
4. For every flagged touchpoint, don't just note "fix voice" — pull one real example line and rewrite it in-voice, and add it to the "say it, don't describe it" table above so the fix is reusable, not a one-off edit.
