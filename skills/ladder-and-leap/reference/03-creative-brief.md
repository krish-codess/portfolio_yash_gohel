# Creative Brief

Most weak creative work traces back to a weak brief, not a weak creative team. A brief that lists five objectives, three audiences, and a proposition that's secretly a paragraph is not a strategic document — it's an unresolved argument the brand manager didn't want to have internally, handed off to someone else to solve under a deadline. This file exists so that argument happens before the brief is written, not after the work comes back wrong.

## The one-page brief template

Keep it to one page. If it doesn't fit, the strategy isn't resolved yet — a longer brief is not a more thorough brief, it's usually a sign the single-minded proposition hasn't actually been chosen.

**Background** — What's the business situation in three sentences or fewer? Why does this need to exist now?

**Objective** — What should happen as a result of this work, stated as a behavior change (buy, switch, try, recommend, trust) — not as a communication goal like "raise awareness," which describes an input, not an outcome.

**Target audience** — Described by the tension or need that makes them receptive to this message right now, not just demographics. "Working mothers 28–40" tells a creative team almost nothing to write toward. "Working mothers who feel guilty every time they choose convenience over what they think a 'good' meal should be" gives them a real human moment to write into.

**Single-minded proposition** — One sentence. The single most important thing the work needs to say. If you cannot compress it to one sentence, you have not finished the strategy — you've moved the unresolved argument from "which audience" to "which proposition," and the creative team will now have to resolve it for you, badly, under deadline.

**Reasons to believe** — The proof that makes the proposition credible. Two or three, ranked by strength, not a complete list of every feature. A reason to believe that requires its own explanation is not doing its job — see the David Abbott approach in `05-copywriting-canon.md` on trusting the reader with real proof rather than a marketing-safe generality.

**Tone and personality** — Reference the voice matrix in `02-brand-voice-system.md` rather than inventing new adjectives per brief. If this brief needs a tone exception (more serious than usual, for instance, because of the subject matter), say so explicitly.

**Mandatories** — Legal lines, logo lockups, claims that must be substantiated, category-specific compliance requirements (especially relevant for health and financial claims — flag anything that will need legal or medical sign-off before the team starts writing, not after).

**Success metrics** — How will you know this worked, and by when? Tie back to the objective's behavior change, not to output metrics like "number of assets produced."

**What good looks like / what to avoid** — One or two reference points each. Real examples (competitor work, past campaigns, even work from a different category that nails the *feeling* you want) communicate more precisely than another paragraph of adjectives.

## The brief-tightness test

Before this brief leaves your hands, run it through these checks:

1. **The one-sentence test.** Can you say the single-minded proposition out loud, from memory, without checking the document? If not, it isn't single-minded yet.
2. **The swap test.** Read the proposition back and mentally swap in a direct competitor's name. If it still sounds true and reasonable for them, the proposition isn't differentiated enough to brief creative work from — it's a category truth, not a brand position.
3. **The audience-in-the-room test.** Would the target audience, reading this brief over your shoulder, recognize themselves in the description, or does it read like a media-planning segment? If they wouldn't recognize themselves, the creative team won't either.
4. **The "so what" test.** For each reason to believe, ask "so what?" once. If the answer isn't obviously connected to the proposition, cut it — a brief padded with weak proof is worse than a brief with one strong reason to believe.
5. **The one-objective test.** Count the verbs in your objective section. If there's more than one behavior change listed ("drive trial and build loyalty and improve perception"), pick one. A brief serving three objectives will produce work that serves none of them well.

## Common brief failure modes

**The feature list disguised as a proposition.** "Our product is fast, affordable, and easy to use" is not a single-minded proposition — it's three propositions wearing a trenchcoat. Force a ranking: which one wins if you can only say one thing?

**The mission statement standing in for a positioning.** "We believe everyone deserves great [category]" describes a company value, not a market position. It doesn't answer which rung of the ladder you're claiming (see `01-positioning.md`) or who you're positioned against.

**Tone described only in adjectives with no reference points.** "Warm, bold, modern, trustworthy" could describe almost any brand in almost any category. Anchor every adjective to either the voice matrix or a real example.

**A brief written to please every internal stakeholder rather than to move one customer.** If the brief reads like it was assembled by adding a sentence for every department's pet concern, it will produce work that satisfies the internal review meeting and fails with the actual audience. The brief's job is to represent the customer in the room, not to represent every internal function.

**No point of view on what to avoid.** A brief that only says what good looks like, with nothing ruled out, leaves the creative team guessing at the boundaries — which usually produces either something too safe (guessing conservatively) or something off-strategy (guessing wrong). One clear "not this" example does more work than a paragraph of positive direction.
